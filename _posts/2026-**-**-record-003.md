## Parenting Ambivalence {#parenting-ambivalence}

This month I read Emily St John Mandel's new release *Exit Party*. I'm a big Mandel fan, having read all of her books. I even got the privilege to see her speak live as part of her book tour for *Exit Party*. 

I could say lots about *Exit Party* but I want to focus on one element that spoke strongly to me as a parent of two young children, and that is around mixed feelings about being a parent. More specifically, how it's possible to both love and hate parenting. This is exemplified most strongly in the character Kareem and his doppelganger (the Nico/Gloria character also fits this mould). The biological father Kareem regrets having children; sees them as a burden, neglects them, distances himself as much as possible. The doppelganger Kareem wishes he had children even though he's uncertain about his ability to be a good parent. 

The two Kareems in the novel represent the kind of inner conflict that I sometimes feel as a parent and that I think a lot of other parents feel too. Let me be clear that to say that one loves *and* hates parenting is not to say that one loves *and* hates their kids — I think you always love your kids even if sometimes it doesn't feel like it in the moment. I'm also not saying that inconsistent parenting feelings are the same as inconsistent parenting *behaviours* (e.g., nurture mixed with neglect). No, I think most parents' behaviours are more or less consistent (or at least should be), but private feelings may vacillate between satisfaction and regret, confidence and uncertainty, acceptance and dismissiveness. 

It's interesting to see similar parental conflictedness appear in Mandel's previous novel *The Sea of Tranquility* where the semi-autobiographical character (whose name escapes me) expresses her anxieties, joys, and self-doubts over her parenting during a pandemic. Of course, I don't know if Mandel herself is projecting her own ambivalence onto her characters, but it feels grounded in a reality that I imagine a lot of parents experience but are reluctant to openly discuss.

For me personally, parenting gives me deep gratification, joy, and wonder; but it is also my primary source of anxiety, stress, and exhaustion. Sometimes I love talking to and playing with my kids; other times I crave time alone, away from them; and still other times I think back to how simple life was before them and a small part of me wishes for that life back, even for just a moment.

There's a moment in the book where the doppelganger Kareem, who has by this point out of mutual agreement assumed the role of parenting *his* doppelganger's kids, is pushing his kids on the playground swings:

> … He [doppelganger Kareem] stood behind and between two swings and pushed both kids ... They loosened up while they were swinging, as if they were creatures meant to live in air, in motion, and he'd returned them to their preferred natural element.

And, I dunno, it's not a huge moment or anything but it fills me with such gratitude of being a parent, of getting to help your kids learn to fly, so to speak. And perhaps it speaks to me especially because my daughter loves the swings and, while pushing her, she'll say gleefully "Higher! Higher! High into the sky!". This image serves as a kind of anchor whenever doubts about my ability or desire to be a parent inevitably creep into my mind. And for a book to do that is a pretty remarkable thing.
