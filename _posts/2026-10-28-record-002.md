## Stoner {#stoner}

I finished reading *Stoner*. I love this book. It's beautiful and tragic in a quiet, stoic kind of way. 

I have a lot of disorganized thoughts about the deeper themes and messages in this novel; thoughts that will probably cohere given time and rereads. 

But one thought I want to share is around the importance of teaching in this book. It helps to understand Stoner's arc a bit: first, humble roots as a farmer, then falling in love with literature, then falling in love with teaching, then falling in love with a student, and finally ending on a solemn realization that it might have all been for nothing, but dying with a shred of hope that he might not be forgotten.

Teaching becomes a sort of calling for Stoner, one that he initially approaches reluctantly and without skill but for which he eventually develops a passion:

> … The love of literature, of language, of the mystery of the mind and heart showing themselves in the minute, strange, and unexpected combinations of letters and words, in the blackest and coldest print — the love which he had hidden as if it were illicit and dangerous, he began to display, tentatively at first, and then boldly, and then proudly.

He wants to share this love literature and he does this through teaching. And this is mirrored in his relationship with his young daughter, who essentially becomes his most cherished student until she is cruelly snatched away from him (emotionally not physically).

The sad thing is that Stoner is thwarted by his wife and his colleagues (namely, Lomax) in all attempts to realize his noble ambitions. And in his parting words at his retirement he can only utter a feeble "thank you for letting me teach". He leaves the academy a pathetic wraith of a man. And he asks himself "What did you expect?" over and over as if there could be nothing more for life to offer other than failed attempts to teach, or perhaps more deeply fail to *connect*, with his students, wife, and daughter.

To teach is to in some way impart a piece of oneself in others — to live on in the lives of one's students through the wisdom that has been passed down; and it would seem that Stoner has failed in this. But in his final moments, something happens that turns this around. And maybe, it wasn't all for nothing — that something of him will persist in this world. Maybe immortality doesn't actually come to be, but the hope itself may be comfort enough.

*Stoner* is one of those books I'll be thinking about for a long time. It has also invigorated my interest in John Williams, and I'll surely be reaching for *Butcher's Crossing* soon, and perhaps even *Augustus* later. As for now, it's onto Hemingway with *For Whom the Bell Tolls*, the next read as part of my book club.

## Eyes are Watching {#eyes-are-watching}

I'm willing to bet if you're a reader you've heard of *Yesteryear*. It's only the buzziest book of 2026. I usually try and pick up one or two of these kinds of books every year - and by 'pick up' I mean metaphorically since I usually do the audiobook from Libby.

So I'm about 2/3 into this, and I'll start by saying that the audiobook production is excellent. Rebecca Lowman, the narrator, really captures the inner workings of our protagonist — a complex admixture of traditional wife/mother and social media influencer, who appears as unlikeable as all get out from the start but toward whom you as the reader slowly develop a fondness or at least understanding.

This is a weird book. Weird in the kind of way that is highly marketable in the current literary climate. And that's not to dismiss it. Setting aside the trendier social media influencer and gameshow leitmotifs, I think this book is doing some pretty interesting thematic work around the concepts of surveillance and performance.

Throughout *Yesteryear* there's a constant thread of someone watching — whether that's a TV audience, hoard of (angry) followers on social media, or even God. As we weave back and forth through time in the 1800s to the present day, we contrast the eyes of God with the more diffuse but no less omnipresent eyes of social media followers. 

The prevailing belief in ye olde times was that God was watching over you, and your good faith would earn you a spot in heaven. In a sense, this is a performance: performing a good life for an omnipotent deity. And now, influencer culture tells us to perform — to adopt a facade of wholesomeness, intellect, power, whatever works to your advantage — not to God, but to an unindividuated and hungry mass of observers.

The novel explores how women in these two different times must deal with these contrasting performative roles in ways that are more challenging and ill-defined than their male counterparts. The traditional Christian wife served her husband and God; the modern Momfluencer serves her followers. In a way, the Momfluencer becomes more akin to the formative God figure (she has 'followers') but the performative lens is still focused squarely over her, a concentrated beam of sunlight.

If we're always performing we can never experience the kind of solitude that is so important to our well-being. Time off stage, as Goffman would put it, is not only restorative but can help us attain a better self-understanding and boost creativity (for more on this, check out Robert Coplan's excellent book *The Joy of Solitude*).

There's a parallel here with *We* by Yevgeny Zamyatin, the 1920s Russian dystopian that preceded *1984* and *Brave New World*. Like in those dystopians, there's a suspicion of solitude and privacy; and so everyone is expected to be on display at all times (apartments are like glass boxes wherein the curtains can only be closed during state-sanctioned periods of intimacy). It's no longer God that is always watching, but the society (in slight contrast with *1984* the emphasis is on people in society monitoring each other rather than the totalitarian state symbolized by Big Brother). And if privacy no longer exists then what does that mean for the individual? for the I? I becomes We.

This performance vs. surveillance thread has been popping up everywhere in my reading this year: *Liberation Day* and *Exit Party*, to name a few. It's also creeping more into my awareness as I learn more about AI Ethics. For me, a big issue when it comes to AI and social media is how they go hand in hand to make us voluntary participants in our own surveillance. We freely give up parts of our lives as data that is then used by AI to feed us back data in the form of ads and other garbage and, perhaps, watch over us. So I'm highly attuned to how fiction, both contemporary and classic, is dealing with the sanctity of the private life.

## No AI {#no-ai}

I work in tech and I use AI every day for work. I use it at home too when I want to look something up or dig deeper into something. I have mixed feelings about AI in my work (e.g., giving up autonomy, sacrificing competency) but I use it because it's productive (or sometimes, let's be honest, I'm lazy and don't want to think through something). 

But where I have zero conflicted feelings is about using AI for my creative writing. I am absolutely 100% against using AI to write because it demeans the genuine creative work that goes into writing. But there's a deeper reason to not use AI here, and that has to do with self-respect. I respect my ideas and my ability to translate those ideas into words. To offload that on an AI would be to disrespect myself.

This gets to the concepts of sincerity and authenticity — two related concepts I've been interested in exploring philosophically for some time. I even have Lionel Trilling's *Sincerity and Authenticity* on my to-read. That said, my layperson's perspective on it is that to create something that comes from yourself is to pursue authenticity, and this, naturally, feels good. In contrast, to leverage AI, even marginally, is to sacrifice that authenticity and take your creative work further away from yourself.

If I were to ever write a book it would be no different. For all the productivity that AI can do in my work space, I want none of it in my creative writing.

(And yes, I use a lot of em-dashes, but hell, that's how I write.)
