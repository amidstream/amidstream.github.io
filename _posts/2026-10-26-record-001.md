## Big Kuhanas {#big-kuhanas}

I swear I don’t normally read so many books concurrently, although I am most definitely a multi-reader. Usually it’s more like four at a time (one print fiction, one print nonfiction, one audiobook, one long-read). But somehow late this year I’ve found myself with eight books midstream… It’s kinda like there are two Big Kuhanas that have been clogging the works for some time: *A People’s Tragedy* and *Ducks, Newburyport*, two books that couldn’t be more different apart from their heft. The Baldwin bio also fits, so we’ll call it three Big Kuhanas (Kuhanae?).

And it’s not like I’m not enjoying these books, although *APT* certainly has some dry sections and *DN* feels adrift and aimless, which, I suppose is the purpose. *A Love Story* is great all around, so far, - a more intimate account of the great civil rights author and activist’s life than the Eddie Glaube Jr book I read some time back.

The point here isn’t to review any of these books. The point is to say that they’re long and it’s causing something of a blockade.

*APT* I can finish this year. *DN* could be a constant companion well into next year; it’s a pretty good one for chipping away at, honestly.

## The “Academe” {#the-academe}

Ah, *Stoner*. A TBR long-hauler that now has me longing for dusty bookshelves, abstruse scholarly literature, and departmental politics. Well, maybe not that last one. In a former life I was an academic and had ambitions of staying in academia forever. It was Psychology not English Literature, but yeah the feeling was more or less the same; perhaps with more computers and fewer musty books. Also, thank goodness, the gender disparity has come a long way since Stoner’s time. 

Well, my academia days are behind me now, suppressed beneath six years working in tech. Don’t get me wrong, at the time I was thrilled to transition to tech (it was 2020, the academic job market reached its all time low - further below what was then seen as an all time low). I was pretty excited back then about coding and “building things”. Now, I yearn for the days where it was considered work to be reading scholarly works.

I’m enjoying this book. The writing has a sparse, forthright quality that is exactly what I needed after having recently read (and not so much enjoyed) *A Picture of Dorian Gray*. One could say the style is maybe a bit too barren - like The Road but with less of an emotional gut punch. Women aren’t portrayed especially well here, either.

But still, it has me fondly recalling my graduate student days and yearning for what might’ve been if I’d’ve stayed in academia.

## Battle of the Grain {#battle-of-the-grain}

I’m 3/4 of the way through *A People’s Tragedy* and the Bolsheviks with Lenin at the helm have attained power. Lenin and Trotsky decide that peasants working the land shouldn’t be able to keep what they reap, rather, everything should be collectivized and redistributed to the people.

Good idea in theory; terrible in practice.

Thus begins the battle of the grain where the peasants do everything they can to squirrel away as much produce and hide it from the state authorities bent on “reclaiming” it. Problem is, the state will take away just about any produce they come across. Doesn’t matter if it’s the last the peasants have to live off of.

The peasants are portrayed as greedy “Kulaks” - the scapegoat for all of Russian society’s woes, including the famine caused by the Bolshevik’s practice of collectivizing the grain.

There’s an echo of this in Zamyatin’s *We* where the narrator - a true believer in the kind of socialist dystopia we see in the novel - says

> … to dominate the world, man had to win a victory over hunger after paying a very high price. I refer to the great Two Hundred Years’ War, the war between the city and the land. Probably on account of religious prejudices, the primitive peasants stubbornly held onto their ‘bread’. … True, only about two tenths of the population of the globe did not die out. But how beautifully shining the face of the earth became when it was cleared of its impurities!

*We* is proving to be a perfect companion to *APT* and I’m glad that I decided to pick it up when I did, even if it contributes to the aforementioned concurrent reading logjam. Definitely getting strong *Brave New World* and *1984* feels. Interesting, of course, that this novel came out before both of those dystopian bulwarks, and yet We doesn’t often get mentioned in the canon.

And yes, *We* is the inspiration behind the approach to these posts: Records defined by three themes (minus the socialist dystopia).
